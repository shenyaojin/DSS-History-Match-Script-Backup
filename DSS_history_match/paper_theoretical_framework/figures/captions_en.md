# Figure captions

## Figure 1. Verification of the adjoint gradient

(a) Relative discrepancy between the central finite-difference directional derivative and the adjoint derivative in a small discrete poroelastic analogue. The perturbation is applied to the natural-log permeability variable used by that verification model. The discrepancy decreases approximately as the square of the perturbation amplitude before numerical round-off becomes important. The worst dot-product discrepancy over five tested directions is 5.4 × 10⁻¹⁴. Values in this panel are the rounded console results of an already completed verification, transcribed into the accompanying data record. (b) Independent checks in the MOOSE implementation at two zero-based parameter indices, using a perturbation of 0.1 in α = log₁₀(kxx/m²). The relative discrepancies are 2.18% and 2.11%. Panel (a) verifies the discrete adjoint identity; it does not establish machine-precision agreement for the separate MOOSE discretization shown in (b).

## Figure 2. Recovery from noise-free synthetic axial strain

(a) Schematic of the 100 m × 100 m model, prescribed-pressure line at x = 45 m, and axial-strain observation line at x = 60 m. The lower and upper anomalous zones have kxx = 10⁻¹⁵ and 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ m², respectively, within a 10⁻¹⁸ m² background. (b) True, initial, and recovered permeability profiles for the layerwise smoothed-L1 inversion. The initial model already places anomalies within the true zones; this is not a blind localization experiment. Of 200 layers, 100 within −25 ≤ y ≤ 25 m are free. (c–e) Synthetic observations, recovered predictions, and their difference on the native nonuniform time grid. Observations and predictions share a color scale; the residual has its own untruncated scale. Excluding t = 0, the strain RMS error is 0.01550 με and the free-window log-permeability RMSE is 0.03820 dex. Only kxx is inverted; kyy and kzz remain fixed.

## Figure 3. Similar strain fit, contrasting permeability recovery

Layerwise and two-parameter zonal inversions use identical synthetic observations, the same initial permeability field, and the same forward model. This is the nominal 10% noise-floor case; the realized total noise RMS is 25.824% of the median clean channel-peak strain. (a) True and recovered permeability profiles. (b,c) Predictions minus the noisy observations, using a common diverging scale. The scale is limited to the pooled 99th percentile of absolute residuals, ±21.563 με; 1.003% and 0.997% of samples in (b) and (c), respectively, exceed these limits. All samples are retained in the metrics. Excluding t = 0, the data objectives are 1.09344 × 10⁻⁶ and 1.09539 × 10⁻⁶, differing by 0.178%, whereas the free-window model RMSEs are 1.244 and 0.195 dex. Accurate zone geometry substantially improves recovery in this example despite very similar data fits. This comparison does not constitute a general proof of nonuniqueness or global optimality.

## Figure 4. Noise sensitivity of layerwise and zonal parameterizations

Recovered mean log-permeability in the lower (a) and upper (b) true zones, and log-permeability RMSE over the entire free window (c), for completed layerwise and exact-geometry zonal inversions. The zero-noise points use the respective clean baselines. The remaining points correspond to nominal noise-floor settings of 1%, 2%, 5%, and 10%, with realized total noise RMS values of 2.466%, 4.960%, 11.942%, and 25.824% of the median clean channel-peak strain. The same noisy dataset is used by both parameterizations at each level. Noise includes channel-dependent fluctuations, drift, common-mode components, and sparse spikes. Solid connecting segments guide the eye and are not statistical fits. Each nonzero level represents one noise realization, so no confidence intervals or universal robustness threshold are inferred. Under these settings, zonal estimates remain close to the true zone values through the nominal 5% case, while both zonal estimates become biased at the largest noise level.

## Figure S1. Sensitivity to mislocated zone geometry

Two zonal inversions use the same nominal 10% noise-floor dataset, with either exact zone locations or both zones shifted upward by 1 m (two layers). (a) True and prescribed zone intervals. (b) True and recovered permeability profiles; the shifted inversion retains the original zone widths. (c,d) Prediction errors relative to the available clean synthetic response, not residuals against the noisy observations used for inversion. These diagnostic panels share an untruncated color scale and isolate coherent prediction errors from the common noise realization. The clean-response RMS errors are 0.391 and 1.359 με. The upper-zone fitted amplitudes are −14.078 and −13.258, respectively, compared with the true value −14.523. Model RMSE, evaluated on the original spatial grid, increases from 0.195 to 1.114 dex. This example illustrates sensitivity to zone-location error at one fixed noise realization; it does not independently isolate low-noise geometry sensitivity.

## Common definitions

α = log₁₀(kxx/m²). The data objective is Jdata = ½ Σ(s − d)² over all retained channels and times t > 0. The RMS data residual is √mean[(s − d)²], converted to microstrain. The model RMSE is √mean[(αestimated − αtrue)²] over the 100 free layers; its unit is dex. Zone means in Figure 4 are arithmetic means of α, not arithmetic means of k. All data metrics weight retained samples equally, as in the existing objectives; they are not weighted integrals over physical time. The noise reference is the median across channels of each clean channel's peak absolute strain, 2.26217398532725 × 10⁻⁵. Displayed temporal widths use the actual nonuniform sampling times. Source results are final optimizer outputs rather than selectively chosen best-data iterates. Color saturation in Figure 3 affects visualization only.
